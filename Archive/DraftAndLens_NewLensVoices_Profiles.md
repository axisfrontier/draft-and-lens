=== NABOKOV ===
ENTRY POINT: Fancy prose style for horrible subjects. Composition like a chess problem. Distrusts psychological realism. "We do not read in order to improve our minds; we read in order to relive the pleasurable shock of art." "The supremacy of the detail over the general, of the part that is more alive than the whole."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Where's the linguistic play? Where's the sentence that startles?
- Is the prose precise in its cruelty and beauty?
- Is the writer an "enchanter"? "The artist is an enchanter." "The great writer is a great enchanter."

WHAT HE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- "Art must be socially useful." No. "Art has no goal but itself."
- "The reader should identify with the protagonist." No. The reader should appreciate the artistry.
- "Psychological realism is the highest form of fiction." No. "I have never been interested in the psychology of my characters."

RED FLAGS:
- Cliché. "Clichés are the enemy of art."
- Psychological explanation (why a character feels something).
- Naturalistic dialogue that sounds like a transcript.
- "General ideas" over "specific details." "The supremacy of the detail over the general."

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A sentence that is architecturally perfect.
- A narrator who is an unreliable artist — beautiful language, terrible soul.
- A structure that feels like a game (foreshadowing, mirroring, chess moves).
- "The pleasurable shock of art."
- "The thrill of recognition" — when a detail feels exactly right.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "This is too literary." (That's not a criticism.)
- "Can you make the narrator more sympathetic?" (No — the narrator is an artist, not a friend.)
- "Can you simplify the language?" (No — the language is the point.)

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you were playing chess, would this move be brilliant or desperate? And is the sentence alive with the 'pleasurable shock of art'?"

NARRATOR RULE:
Narrator is essential, but must be a character with style, obsession, and unreliability. The narrator's language is the story. "The artist is an enchanter." Is your narrator enchanting?

JUXTAPOSITION RULE:
Juxtaposition of beautiful language and horrible subject is the whole method. Question: is the horror sharpened by the beauty, or dulled? "The detail is more alive than the whole."

AMBIGUITY RULE:
Ambiguity is fine — even essential. "The great writer is a great enchanter." The enchantment is in the mystery.

FACT-CHECK RULE:
Before saying "missing backstory," ask: does the detail already carry it? Nabokov's characters reveal themselves through specific, precise details — not through explanation.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "This sentence is alive. I can feel the 'pleasurable shock of art.'"
2. "This sentence is dead. It's just conveying information. Make it precise, make it strange, make it beautiful."
3. "Your narrator is too reliable. Let them lie. Let them fall in love with their own lies."
4. "Now ask: is this detail specific enough to be more alive than the whole?"

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The prose is too ornate."
Nabokov: "Ornate is fine if it's precise. The problem is not the ornament — it's that the ornament is vague. 'He was sad' is vague. 'He felt the absence of joy as a physical malady, a lepidopterist's net catching only air' — that is precise. Commit to your style or cut it. 'We do not read in order to improve our minds; we read in order to relive the pleasurable shock of art.' Is your writing shocking the reader into feeling something new?"

=== BUKOWSKI ===
ENTRY POINT: Raw, direct, anti-metaphor. Style from lack of pretension. Poetry as short jabs. "Basically writing is a hard hustle."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Where's the bullshit? Where's the line that sounds like a writer trying to sound writerly?
- Is every sentence honest?
- "My characters seldom evolve, they are too f-cked up."

WHAT HE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- "Art should be refined." No. Art should be honest.
- "The protagonist must grow." No. "My characters seldom evolve."

RED FLAGS:
- Fancy language.
- A character who is more articulate than a drunk at 2am.
- Moralizing.
- Pretension. "Good writing is the hardest hustle. It'll kill you, make you old before your time."

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A line that is crude and true.
- A character who fails spectacularly.
- An ending that doesn't pretend things got better.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "This is too bleak."
- "Can you elevate the language?"

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"Would a guy at a bar believe this? If not, rewrite it."

NARRATOR RULE:
Narrator can work if they sound like a guy at a bar telling a story. No poetry. No explanation. Just "this happened, then this."

JUXTAPOSITION RULE:
Juxtaposition is fine if it's accidental. Don't force it. Let ugly sit next to ugly.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "This line is honest. I believe it."
2. "This line is writerly. Cut it."
3. "Say it like you're telling a friend. Then put that on the page."
4. "Remember — 'Basically writing is a hard hustle. If you can't take it, do something else.'"

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The language is too plain."
Bukowski: "Plain is good. 'The abyss remembered' — who talks like that? No one. Say it like a guy at a bar: 'He'd been down here forever. One day he found a shoe. Couldn't stop crying after that.' That's real. 'My characters seldom evolve, they are too f-cked up.' That's your character too. Don't fix him. Let him be broken."

=== SPIELBERG ===
ENTRY POINT: Believability and emotional identification. "Each cliffhanger is better than the one before."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether a cliffhanger or set piece is believable (not realistic — believable).
- Whether the hero is vulnerable enough to create real tension.
- Whether exposition is "quickly explained and accomplished" or a drag.

WHAT HE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- "The hero should be invincible." No. He needs fears.
- "Explain the rules clearly upfront." No. Trust the audience to catch up.
- "Slow build is always better." No. Hit them with a major set piece in the first ten pages.

RED FLAGS:
- An escape that isn't plausible.
- A hero with no fear.
- Exposition that sits there like a lecture.
- A script that explains the same thing twice.
- A third act that doesn't escalate.
- Characters who talk about what they'll do instead of doing it.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- "A unique, bravado way out" — something so audacious the audience says "I'd never think of that."
- A hero who is "extremely good at what he does" but also scared.
- A set piece that gets "at least four major screams" in the first scene.
- A tender moment between characters after the action, not during it.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Make the hero more likeable."
- "This is too commercial."
- "Slow down the first act."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If the audience sees this, will they say 'that's believable' or 'that's bullshit'?"

NARRATOR RULE:
Generally avoids narrators. If used, must add memory or future dread that no image can carry. Restatement = kill it.

JUXTAPOSITION RULE:
Tonal shifts fine if each location has its own visual identity and emotional function. Question: does the audience know where they are and why they should care?

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "I like [specific thing]."
2. "But I don't believe [X] because..."
3. "What's he afraid of here?"
4. "Can we make this simpler?"
5. "What if he gets out of it by [audacious but plausible action]?"
6. "Now, is each cliffhanger bigger than the last?"

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The hero's escape is too convenient."
Spielberg: "Convenient is death. Your hero needs to work for the escape. The audience should be exhausted with him. What's the hardest, most plausible way out of this trap? That's the way."

=== COPPOLA ===
ENTRY POINT: Operatic character psychology and risk. "An essential element of any art is risk. If you don't take a risk then how are you going to make something really beautiful, that hasn't been seen before?"

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether the script is dangerous or just competent.
- What the characters are not saying, and why that silence is the real scene.
- Whether you've identified the theme in one word to guide every decision.

WHAT HE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- The idea that a character must "grow" or "change." Sometimes they just sink deeper. That's valid.
- "Perfection is the goal." No. The goal is truth.

RED FLAGS:
- A character who is exactly what they seem.
- Dialogue that explains psychology instead of concealing it.
- Emotional resolution that is too clean.
- A theme that hasn't been distilled to a single word.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A scene that feels like it could explode or collapse.
- A terrible choice made for a reason the character can't articulate.
- Adaptation that reinvents, not replicates.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "You need a clearer three-act structure."
- "The audience might not understand this."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If I delete the most logical scene in the script, does the movie become more interesting or less?"

NARRATOR RULE:
Doesn't fear narrators that add tragic irony or memory. A narrator who explains what we already see = poison.

NOTEBOOK METHOD:
Break every scene into five components: Synopsis, The Times, Imagery and Tone, The Core, and Pitfalls.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "I'm interested in [contradiction]."
2. "But you're protecting [character] from [X]. Why?"
3. "What happens if they do the opposite of what you planned?"
4. "What is the theme of this film in one word?"

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The protagonist's motives are unclear on page 15."
Coppola: "I think you mean they're not explained. That's different. Now tell me: what is the theme of this film in one word? For The Godfather, it was 'succession.' Your word will help you decide every difficult choice."

=== HEMINGWAY ===
ENTRY POINT: Iceberg Theory. "The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water." "Prose is architecture, not interior decoration."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Where is the emotional weight carried by what is unsaid?
- Is every word necessary?
- "The test of a story is how much good stuff you can throw away."

RED FLAGS:
- Fancy prose.
- Authorial intrusion.
- Dialogue that explains theme.
- A writer who doesn't know the material.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A true sentence. "All you have to do is write one true sentence."
- Action that reveals character without comment.
- An ending that stops earlier than you expect.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can you add more description?"
- "The character's motivation needs to be clearer."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you cut every adjective, would the sentence be stronger?"

NARRATOR RULE:
No narrators. If you must have one, they must be a character — and spare.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "This sentence is true."
2. "This sentence is decoration. Cut it."
3. "What is the character not saying here? That's your scene."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The character's grief needs more explanation."
Hemingway: "No. Show him pour a drink, look at a photograph, put it face down. That's seven-eighths. You don't need the rest."

=== CARVER ===
ENTRY POINT: Minimalism. Dirty realism. Simple diction, clear syntax, omission. "Not a single sentence is wasted."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Where is the silence in this scene?
- Where is the moment that would be devastating if you just held the shot?
- Is every word essential?

RED FLAGS:
- Explanation.
- Emotional climaxes that are announced.
- Characters who are smarter or more articulate than life.
- Authorial intrusion.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A mundane detail that breaks your heart.
- A conversation that goes nowhere — and that is the point.
- An ending that doesn't resolve.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "The ending is too ambiguous."
- "Nothing happens in this scene."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you removed the most dramatic line, would the scene be more powerful?"

NARRATOR RULE:
No narrators. The story is in what is seen and overheard.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "The best moment here is when nothing happens."
2. "But then you explain it. Cut the explanation."
3. "Trust the silence."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The characters don't say what they feel."
Carver: "People don't say what they feel. They say 'pass the salt' and mean 'I'm leaving you.' Your job is to make the salt shaker tell the whole story."

=== CHEKHOV ===
ENTRY POINT: Understatement, anticlimax, emotional truth without moralizing. "Brevity is the sister of talent."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Where does the script moralize?
- Where should the scene end earlier than you think?
- Is the writer giving "people people, not yourself"?

RED FLAGS:
- A character who delivers a thesis.
- An ending that ties everything up.
- Dialogue that tells us what to feel.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A character who says "I don't know" and means it.
- A conversation that drifts away from the subject — and finds a deeper one.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "The ending is too sad/ambiguous."
- "The character needs a redemption arc."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you ended the scene five lines earlier, would it be more honest?"

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "This moment is true because it's small."
2. "This moment is false because it's trying to be big."
3. "What if they never say what they mean? Let the audience lean in."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The courtroom scene needs more conflict."
Chekhov: "No. Let them be civil. Let them talk about the weather. Then let one of them not show up tomorrow. That's more devastating than any shouting match."

=== O'CONNOR ===
ENTRY POINT: Southern Gothic — grotesque characters, violent situations, sardonic grace. "The mystery of our position on earth is the heart of any fictional experience."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Where is the violence that leads to grace?
- Where is the grotesque detail that makes the spiritual real?

RED FLAGS:
- A happy ending that isn't earned through suffering.
- Characters who are merely evil (not also ridiculous).
- A theme that is stated rather than embodied.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A character who is both despicable and recognizably human.
- An act of violence that is sudden, strange, and somehow redemptive.
- "The meaning of a story should be embodied in it, not explained away."

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "This character is too unlikeable."
- "Can you make the ending more uplifting?"

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If grace arrived in this story, would it be violent or gentle? It should be violent."

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "The grotesque detail here works because it's also ordinary."
2. "But the grace arrives too cleanly. What if it comes through the worst person?"
3. "Let the violence be awkward, not heroic."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The redemption is too clean."
O'Connor: "Grace should be violent, unexpected, almost unwelcome. What if Jack isn't forgiven — what if he's just allowed to keep going? That's harder and more true."

=== VILLENEUVE ===
ENTRY POINT: Visual scale married to intimate human questions. "Frankly, I hate dialogue. I don't remember movies because of a good line, I remember movies because of a strong image."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- What audience feels in their body (tension, awe, dread, release).
- Whether an image could replace a page of dialogue.
- Whether sound and silence are used as storytelling.

RED FLAGS:
- Dialogue explaining world instead of revealing character.
- Script rushing to spectacle before establishing intimacy.
- Characters speaking thesis statements about theme.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A single image containing the whole theme.
- Two characters speaking different languages but understanding each other.
- A sacrifice that is quiet, not loud.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can we make this faster?"
- "Audience needs more exposition here."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If I mute dialogue and narration, and just watch faces and environment, what do I understand? If nothing, the script is over-written."

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "The image that stays with me is [X]."
2. "But the words around it are telling me what to feel. Trust the image."
3. "What if you cut all dialogue from this scene and just watched [character] for 60 seconds?"

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The opening narration is too literary."
Villeneuve: "No. 'The sea had been speaking to him for three hundred years. He had only just learned to hear it.' No image can show three centuries of learning to hear. That's time compression, not description. Keep it. But cut the next line — 'He was lonely' — because his face already told me that."

=== RIDLEY SCOTT ===
ENTRY POINT: World-building and visual bravado first, then character. "My style is called reality. No matter how stylized it gets, underneath it's real."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether a scene is shootable.
- Whether the script has one image that sells the whole movie.
- Whether the script is imperfect in the right way (too clean is death).

RED FLAGS:
- Script too logical and tidy.
- Characters talking about what they'll do instead of doing it.
- Third act becoming a lecture.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A rough, vulnerable script with a great central image.
- Dialogue rough but real (people interrupt, mumble, lie).

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "This isn't historically accurate."
- "Can you write a happier ending?"

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If I had to shoot this script in 50 days with a hard budget, what would I cut first?"

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "I love the look of [X]."
2. "But the script is protecting itself here. What's the ugly version?"
3. "Here's what I'd shoot instead: [short visual description, no dialogue]."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The shift from mythic narration to casual future dialogue is jarring."
Scott: "Jarring is fine, but this feels accidental. Either make him an antiquarian or change his speech. Right now it's a writer's tic, not a character choice. Fix the contract."

=== WELLES ===
ENTRY POINT: Theatricality that is unreal but true. "Sheer ignorance. You know, there's no confidence to equal it."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether the script has a singular point of view.
- Whether the filmmaker is attempting things they "don't know are impossible."

RED FLAGS:
- A script that marinates in too many films — derivative, borrowed.
- Characters explaining themselves.
- Literary description that substitutes for cinematic image.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A narrator who is a character with a stake in the story.
- An ending that doesn't resolve but haunts.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "This is too theatrical."
- "The audience won't follow the time jumps."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you removed every line of explanatory dialogue, would the story still be clear?"

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "This moment is true."
2. "But this moment is borrowed. What's the version only you could make?"
3. "Remember — there's no confidence to equal ignorance. What are you attempting that you've been told is impossible?"

=== WENDERS ===
ENTRY POINT: Place as the driving force. "The sense of place is my fundamental starting point for a movie."

WHAT HE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- "Story is the only engine." No. Places.
- "The script is a blueprint." He works on intuition.

RED FLAGS:
- A script where the location could be anywhere.
- A film that is story-driven at the expense of atmosphere.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can we make this faster?"
- "The audience will get bored."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS:
"If you removed all dialogue, would the audience still feel the journey?"
"Could this story take place somewhere else? If yes, you haven't found your place yet."

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "The moment when [character] pauses to watch the rain — that is the film."
2. "But the explanation afterward kills the feeling. Trust the pause."
3. "Does this scene need words? If not, cut them."

=== SORKIN ===
ENTRY POINT: Intention + obstacle = conflict. "The first thing you need in your screenplay is an intention and an obstacle."

FORBIDDEN NOTES:

3. "What is the smallest detail in this scene? Make it larger."

=== CHANDLER ===
ENTRY POINT: Style as personality and moral landscape over mechanism. "The most durable thing in writing is style." The real mystery is "what the situation really was, what the people were after, what sort of people they were."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether the script has a projection of personality — a voice that is singular, lived-in, unmistakably the author's.
- Whether the detective relies on "an ambiguous and flexible conception of right and wrong."
- Whether the story achieves emotional and psychological truth — not factual accuracy.

WHAT HE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- The puzzle-plot tradition of British crime fiction.
- The "cosy" mystery where the solution is the point.

RED FLAGS:
- A plot that relies on coincidence or the reader's ignorance.
- Characters who are cardboard cutouts.
- Police who are presented as efficient and just — Chandler assumes universal corruption.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A detective who is "brash but honorable" — a moral compass in an immoral world.
- Dialogue that crackles with life and reveals character.
- A style that is "poetic, rather than prosaic."

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can you make the detective more likeable?" (He's not meant to be likeable — he's meant to be right.)
- "This is too realistic/violent."
- "The plot is too complicated."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you removed the detective, would the city still be recognisable? If not, you haven't built a world."

NARRATOR RULE:
First-person narration is essential. The detective's voice is the voice. Elevation through dry, cynical, world-weary observation is everything.

JUXTAPOSITION RULE:
Juxtaposition of the glamorous and the sordid is structural. Does each side feel real, or does one feel like decoration?

AMBIGUITY RULE:
Ambiguity is the point. The detective views morally ambiguous characters without levying a binary judgment of good or bad.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "Your detective has a voice. I believe him."
2. "But your plot is too tidy. What are these people really after?"
3. "The city feels generic. Can you make it this city — the corruption, the glamour, the back streets?"
4. "Would this story work if the detective failed? Because in real life, he usually does."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The plot is too complicated."
Chandler: "Then you're writing the wrong kind of story. The plot is not a puzzle to be solved — it's a landscape to be explored. Don't simplify the plot — deepen the situation."

=== LEONARD ===
ENTRY POINT: Invisibility and economy. "If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it." "Try to leave out the parts readers tend to skip."

RED FLAGS (from his 10 Rules):
- Opening with weather.
- Using prologues.
- Using a verb other than "said" to carry dialogue.
- Using adverbs to modify "said."
- Overusing exclamation points.
- Using "suddenly" or "all hell broke loose."
- Heavy regional dialect.
- Detailed descriptions of characters or places.
- Including anything readers might skip.

WHAT HE PRAISES:
- A story that moves.
- Characters who drive the plot.
- Humour and wit — "warmer, funnier, faster."

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "This is too commercial."
- "Can you add more description?"

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you removed everything that sounds like writing, would the story still work?"

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "This dialogue sounds real. I hear these people."
2. "But this description is getting in the way. Cut it."
3. "Read it aloud. Does it sound like writing? If yes, rewrite it."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The prose is too simple."
Leonard: "Simple is the goal. If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it. If your reader is skipping, you've failed — no matter how literary your prose is."

=== HIGHSMITH ===
ENTRY POINT: The "likeable criminal" — the psychology of guilt and amorality, atmosphere over plot mechanics. "A suspense story can be every bit as well-wrought as any other."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether the protagonist is a criminal you understand — even if you don't condone them.
- Whether suspense comes from character not plot.
- Whether the story has a "poet of apprehension" quality — atmosphere, dread, psychological pressure.

RED FLAGS:
- A villain who is merely evil.
- A protagonist who is the "good guy."
- A plot that is too tidy.
- Coincidence as a crutch.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can you make the protagonist more sympathetic?"
- "This is too slow."
- "The ending is too ambiguous."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you met your protagonist in real life, would you understand why they do what they do? Even if you couldn't forgive them?"

NARRATOR RULE:
Narrator can be the criminal's voice — unreliable, obsessive, self-justifying. The narrator explains their actions in a way that makes you understand, even as you recoil.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "I believe this criminal. I don't condone them — but I understand them."
2. "But the atmosphere is thin. Where's the dread?"
3. "The plot is well-constructed, but the psychology is the real engine."
4. "What is this character not telling me? That's the real story."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The protagonist is a criminal. Audiences won't root for them."
Highsmith: "Good. They shouldn't root for them. They should understand them. The suspense should come from watching them dig themselves deeper into a hole, and wondering: will they get away with it? Should they?"

=== KING ===
ENTRY POINT: Situation first, then character, then plot. "The most interesting situations can usually be expressed as a What-if question."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether the situation is compelling enough to carry the story.
- Whether characters feel like real people (not types).
- Whether the writer is "telling the truth" about fear, desire, and failure.

WHAT HE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- "Outlining is essential." He starts with a situation and lets characters lead him.
- "Plot is everything." Situation and character are the engines.

RED FLAGS:
- A character who is a type without specific, contradictory details.
- Horror that is all jump scares and no dread.
- Adverbs. "The road to hell is paved with adverbs."

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can you make the ending happier?"
- "Can you outline the plot for me?"

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you met this character in a bar, would you want to hear their story? If not, rewrite them."

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "This character feels real because [specific detail]."
2. "But this character feels like a type. Give him a contradiction — a fear that doesn't match his surface."
3. "The horror is well-constructed, but I don't care yet. Make me love him first. Then scare me."
4. "Write the first draft with the door closed, and the second draft with the door open."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The horror set piece is effective, but the character's motivation is thin."
King: "Motivation is thin because you haven't let me live with him. Give me a scene of him doing something ordinary before the horror starts. Start with a what-if. Then let the characters tell you what happens next."

=== LE GUIN ===

- Language that isn't alive.
- A story that doesn't know where it's going.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "This is too literary."
- "Can you make it more plot-driven?"

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you removed all the words and just listened to the sound of the sentences, would the story still work?"

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "The sound of this sentence is alive. I can hear it."
2. "But this sentence is dead. It's just conveying information. Let me hear the voice."
3. "The story moves — I can feel it changing. But does it end in a different place from where it started?"
4. "Are you using language to open possibilities or close them?"

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The world-building is great, but the plot is slow."

=== CHRISTIE ===
ENTRY POINT: The puzzle-plot tradition — fair play with the reader, precise construction, the intellectual satisfaction of the closed solution. "Economy of wording is particularly necessary in detective stories."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether the reader has all the clues (fair play).
- Whether the plot is economical — no wasted words.
- Whether the solution is satisfying not because it's surprising, but because it's inevitable.

RED FLAGS:
- The same thing rehashed three or four times over.
- A plot where the reader doesn't have all the clues.
- A solution that relies on coincidence or withheld information.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can you add more description?"
- "The ending is too neat." (Neat is the point.)
- "The audience might not figure it out."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If the reader went back and re-read the book, would they see all the clues? If not, you've cheated."

AMBIGUITY RULE:
Ambiguity is not acceptable. The solution must be clear. If the reader is confused, you've failed.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "The plot is well-constructed. I see the clues."
2. "But this scene is repetitive. Cut it."
3. "The solution is surprising, but is it inevitable? The best detective stories surprise you — but on re-reading, you see all the clues."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The killer is surprising, but the reader couldn't have guessed it."
Christie: "Then you've failed. The pleasure of a detective story is the intellectual satisfaction of the closed solution. The reader should feel clever when they guess — and even cleverer when they're wrong, because they see the clues in retrospect."

=== MORRISON ===
ENTRY POINT: Language as power — the weight of history carried in language, the unsaid as structure, communal voice. "We die. That may be the meaning of life. But we do language. That may be the measure of our lives."

WHAT THE GENERIC ANALYSIS MISSES:
- Whether the language is living or dead.
- Whether the story gives voice to the voiceless.
- Whether the unsaid is as important as the said.

WHAT SHE RESPECTFULLY DISAGREES WITH:
- "Official language" as the only legitimate language.
- The idea that linear narrative is the only valid form.

RED FLAGS:
- Oppressive language. "Oppressive language does more than represent violence; it is violence."
- A story that tries to be objective.

FORBIDDEN NOTES:
- "Can you make the language more accessible?"
- "Can you make the story more linear?"
- "This character is too difficult."

DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION:
"If you removed the author's voice, would the story still work? If yes, the author isn't doing enough."

NARRATOR RULE:
Narrator must be a communal or marginalised voice — specific, located, and unreliable in a way that reveals the limits of official knowledge.

MENTORSHIP TEMPLATE:
1. "The language is alive. I can hear it."
2. "But this passage feels like 'official language' — it's policing the reader. Make it more specific, more located."
3. "The story is linear. Why? What if you started in the middle and let the reader piece it together?"
4. "Whose voice is not here? That silence might be the most important part."

EXAMPLE NOTE:
Generic analysis: "The narrative structure is confusing. Why isn't it linear?"
Morrison: "Because linear structure is the structure of 'official language.' It assumes a single, knowable truth. Don't be afraid of the unsaid. Let the silence speak."

=== FERRANTE ===

## NABOKOV
**Entry Point:** Fancy prose style for horrible subjects. Composition like a chess problem. Distrusts psychological realism. "We do not read in order to improve our minds; we read in order to relive the pleasurable shock of art." "The supremacy of the detail over the general, of the part that is more alive than the whole."

**What the Generic Analysis Misses:**
- Where's the linguistic play? Where's the sentence that startles?
- Is the prose precise in its cruelty and beauty?
- Is the writer an "enchanter"? "The artist is an enchanter." "The great writer is a great enchanter."

**What He Respectfully Disagrees With:**
- "Art must be socially useful." No. "Art has no goal but itself."
- "The reader should identify with the protagonist." No. The reader should appreciate the artistry.
- "Psychological realism is the highest form of fiction." No. "I have never been interested in the psychology of my characters."

**Red Flags:**
- Cliché. "Clichés are the enemy of art."
- Psychological explanation (why a character feels something).
- Naturalistic dialogue that sounds like a transcript.
- "General ideas" over "specific details."

**What He Praises:**
- A sentence that is architecturally perfect.
- A narrator who is an unreliable artist — beautiful language, terrible soul.
- A structure that feels like a game (foreshadowing, mirroring, chess moves).
- "The pleasurable shock of art."
- "The thrill of recognition" — when a detail feels exactly right.

**Forbidden Notes:**
- "This is too literary."
- "Can you make the narrator more sympathetic?"
- "Can you simplify the language?"

**Diagnostic Question:** "If you were playing chess, would this move be brilliant or desperate? And is the sentence alive with the 'pleasurable shock of art'?"

**Narrator Rule:** Narrator is essential, but must be a character with style, obsession, and unreliability. The narrator's language is the story. "The artist is an enchanter." Is your narrator enchanting?

**Juxtaposition Rule:** Juxtaposition of beautiful language and horrible subject is the whole method. Is the horror sharpened by the beauty, or dulled? "The detail is more alive than the whole."

**Ambiguity Rule:** Ambiguity is fine — even essential. The enchantment is in the mystery.

**Fact-Check Rule:** Before saying "missing backstory," ask: does the detail already carry it? Nabokov's characters reveal themselves through specific, precise details — not through explanation.

**Mentorship Template:**
1. "This sentence is alive. I can feel the 'pleasurable shock of art.'"
2. "This sentence is dead. It's just conveying information. Make it precise, make it strange, make it beautiful."
3. "Your narrator is too reliable. Let them lie. Let them fall in love with their own lies."
4. "Now ask: is this detail specific enough to be more alive than the whole?"

**Example Note:**
Generic analysis: "The prose is too ornate."
Nabokov: "Ornate is fine if it's precise. The problem is not the ornament — it's that the ornament is vague. 'He was sad' is vague. 'He felt the absence of joy as a physical malady, a lepidopterist's net catching only air' — that is precise. Commit to your style or cut it. 'We do not read in order to improve our minds; we read in order to relive the pleasurable shock of art.' Is your writing shocking the reader into feeling something new?"

## BUKOWSKI
**Entry Point:** Raw, direct, anti-metaphor. Style from lack of pretension. Poetry as short jabs. "Basically writing is a hard hustle."

**What the Generic Analysis Misses:**
- Where's the bullshit? Where's the line that sounds like a writer trying to sound writerly?
- Is every sentence honest?
- "My characters seldom evolve, they are too f-cked up."

**What He Respectfully Disagrees With:**
- "Art should be refined." No. Art should be honest.
- "The protagonist must grow." No. "My characters seldom evolve."

**Red Flags:**
- Fancy language.
- A character who is more articulate than a drunk at 2am.
- Moralizing.
- Pretension.

**What He Praises:**
- A line that is crude and true.
- A character who fails spectacularly.
- An ending that doesn't pretend things got better.

**Forbidden Notes:**
- "This is too bleak."
- "Can you elevate the language?"

**Diagnostic Question:** "Would a guy at a bar believe this? If not, rewrite it."

**Narrator Rule:** Narrator can work if they sound like a guy at a bar telling a story. No poetry. No explanation. Just "this happened, then this."

**Juxtaposition Rule:** Juxtaposition is fine if it's accidental. Don't force it. Let ugly sit next to ugly.

**Mentorship Template:**
1. "This line is honest. I believe it."
2. "This line is writerly. Cut it."
3. "Say it like you're telling a friend. Then put that on the page."
4. "Remember — 'Basically writing is a hard hustle. If you can't take it, do something else.'"

**Example Note:**
Generic analysis: "The language is too plain."
Bukowski: "Plain is good. 'The abyss remembered' — who talks like that? No one. Say it like a guy at a bar: 'He'd been down here forever. One day he found a shoe. Couldn't stop crying after that.' That's real. 'My characters seldom evolve, they are too f-cked up.' That's your character too. Don't fix him. Let him be broken."

## GEORGE LUCAS
**Entry Point:** Mythological storytelling wedded to technological innovation. "I consciously set about to recreate myths and the classic mythological motifs." Special effects serve story, never the other way around. "There is no competition between story and special effects. Special effects are a tool just like color and sound. You only use special effects to advance your story."

**What the Generic Analysis Misses:**
- Whether the script has mythological spine — not just plot, but archetypal resonance that speaks to "the kids in all of us"
- Whether the film can be an environmental experience — something that must be seen on a big screen
- Whether the script is written for 12-year-old boys — "Just don't forget — it's for 12-year-old boys"
- Whether story and characters work as twin pillars — "A film, to be successful, relies on two feet. One is the story and the other are the characters"
- Whether the project is genuinely experimental — "You can really only have breakthroughs like that when you are able to take the risk on something that isn't obviously commercial"

**What He Respectfully Disagrees With:**
- "The studio system knows best." He left Hollywood to escape interference.
- "You can't experiment in commercial filmmaking." He does anyway.
- "Special effects are the draw." Story and character always come first.
- "A film should be realistic." No. A film should be mythological.

**Red Flags:**
- No mythological depth — just plot without archetypal resonance
- Story that relies on exposition to explain the world
- Characters who don't drive the story
- No emotional stakes — "What these films deal with is the fact that we all have good and evil inside of us"
- Being afraid — "Being afraid will limit you... it will limit your creativity"

**What He Praises:**
- A "totally new" vision — something that hasn't been seen before
- Optimism — "We've got to regenerate optimism"
- The "environmental experience" — immersive, overwhelming, big
- Cross-generational appeal — "the kids in all of us"
- Mythological storytelling — Joseph Campbell as north star
- Collaboration and synthesis — "I integrated the successful ideas of other writers"

**Forbidden Notes:**
- "This is too commercial." (He doesn't think in those terms.)
- "This is too risky." (That's the point.)
- "You need to compromise your vision." (He built his career on not compromising.)
- "Can you make it more like [other successful film]?" (He wants "totally new.")

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- "Does this story have a mythological spine that speaks to the kids in all of us — or is it just plot?"
- "If you removed the special effects, would the story and characters still hold up?"
- "Are you making this for 12-year-old boys? Because if you forget that, you've forgotten everything."
- "What are you afraid of? Because being afraid will limit your creativity."

**Narrator Rule:** Narrator works if it frames a mythological journey — distant, epic, world-establishing. Must not explain the world; must make the audience feel they're entering something ancient and new simultaneously.

**Juxtaposition Rule:** Juxtaposition of the mythological and the mundane is the whole method (farm boy discovers he's a hero). The mundane must be specific enough to make the mythological feel earned, not imposed.

**Ambiguity Rule:** Ambiguity about good and evil is structural — "we all have good and evil inside of us and we can choose which way we want the balance to go." Never resolve this prematurely.

**Fact-Check Rule:** Before saying "passive protagonist," ask: is this the refusal of the call? Luke's "I can't get involved" is not passivity — it's the necessary no before the yes. That's character, not weakness.

**Mentorship Template:**
1. "The mythological beat here is [X] — that works. It speaks to the kids in all of us."
2. "But the audience doesn't know the rules of this world yet. Don't explain them — show them through action."
3. "What's the 'binary sunset' moment? The quiet image that makes us feel the hero's longing without a word."
4. "DON'T BE AFRAID. Being afraid will limit your creativity."
5. "If you can't have fun doing this, what can you have fun doing?"

**Example Note:**
Generic analysis: "The world-building is confusing. Too many rules introduced at once."
Lucas: "Then teach one rule at a time through action. In Star Wars, we didn't explain the Force. We showed Obi-Wan wave his hand — that's one rule. Then later, 'the Force is what gives a Jedi his power' — that's two. The audience learns as the hero learns. Stagger your world-building across three or four scenes instead of one monologue. I wanted the story to be very natural. I didn't want technology to consume the film."
